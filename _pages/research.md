## Publications

**Local Favoritism in State Formation**. with Se Yan and Zhengkai Yang, *Journal of Economic History*, forthcoming.

<details class="paper-abstract">
  <summary>Abstract</summary>
  <p>
    An unconstrained strong state can lead to catastrophic outcomes, underscoring the importance of local flexibility in policy implementation. During China’s land reform, southern counties had both cadres from the central government and local cadres, and they showed different attitudes in implementing policies. Using land ruggedness as an instrument, we find that a 10 percentage point increase in the proportion of local cadres led to a 0.8% reduction in the share of people classified as exploiters, suggesting more lenient policy implementation. We further find that this local favoritism partially explains regional differences in mortality during the Great Famine.
  </p>
</details>

## Working Papers

**Buying Stability: Civil Service Quotas and Elite Co-optation in Imperial China**. with Se Yan

<details class="paper-abstract">
  <summary>Abstract</summary>
  <p>
    How do autocratic states buy political stability from elites, and what are the long-run costs? This paper examines the social and political consequences of the 1427 regional quota reform in China’s civil service examination system. Using a prefecture-level difference-in-differences design, we show that quota expansion increased social mobility and exam access, but lowered admission quality. The additional quotas were disproportionately acquired by the prefectures historically strong in education and local elites, suggesting elite co-optation. Quota expansion reduced elite-led rebellions in the short run, while having little effect on peasant uprisings. Extending the analysis to the entire Ming dynasty, we find a dynamic reversal pattern. Regions receiving larger quota increases experienced more peasant uprisings in the late Ming, and this pattern remains robust to alternative prefecture-level exposure measures. Place-based quotas helped the state buy elite stability, but this political bargain may have shifted the costs of maintaining order onto ordinary peasants and led to long-run popular instability.
  </p>
</details>

**The Daughters of Disaster: Famine, Gender Preferences, and Cohort Sex Ratios in China**. *Reject and Resubmit at Journal of Population Economics*

<details class="paper-abstract">
  <summary>Abstract</summary>
  <p>
    Disasters can reshape social preferences in ways that persist long after the immediate crisis has passed. This paper studies whether China’s Great Famine, from 1959 to 1961, durably shifted family preferences toward daughters. Using prefecture-by-cohort sex ratios from the 1990 Census, I find that famine severity predicts lower sex ratios primarily among post-famine cohorts born from 1963 to 1970, not only among famine-born cohorts. This pattern is robust to alternative famine measures, province exclusions, and migration-restricted samples. Fertility histories show that women from harder-hit areas reported fewer sons among both births and surviving children, with postnatal losses rising more for boys. A nationally representative social survey reveals that respondents in more affected areas express stronger daughter preferences, without a corresponding shift toward broader gender egalitarianism. The results suggest that extreme deprivation can alter household child-gender valuation, weakening son preference without necessarily transforming gender ideology.
  </p>
</details>
